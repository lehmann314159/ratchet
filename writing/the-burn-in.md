The first three pieces cover [what the system is](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/168dda2b-2945-41dc-920e-abc5458b22a4), [how it's built](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/79e0d9bd-aa5b-4b84-ac34-690c4e1d9f31), and [how a design doc has to be written](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/3638f7a6-2456-4d16-b69c-852b0756ad93) for it. This one is about the week the team stopped building it and just ran it, honestly, to find out whether all of that actually holds up.*

## The one rule that makes any of this worth reading

A burn-in, in the sense meant here, is a deliberate stress-test with a single non-negotiable constraint: nothing gets fixed while it's running. Every bug, stall, and escalation gets logged and the corpus moves on — recovered automatically if the framework's own recovery machinery handles it, escalated to a human if it doesn't — but nobody reaches in and patches anything mid-run. That constraint is the entire point. A framework that's been nursed along by someone quietly intervening every time something looks wrong tells you nothing about how it behaves unattended, which is the only way it's ever actually going to run.

The corpus: **nine design docs**, spanning from small self-contained utilities (a decimal-formatting library, a TOML parser) up through genuinely integrated systems (a cron-expression studio, an L-system generator with a live rendering studio, a task list web app). Each doc ran **twice**, with a third tie-breaker run seeded whenever the first two runs disagreed about whether the same doc would complete cleanly. Eighteen planned runs plus five tie-breakers came to **twenty-three total project runs** — roughly seventy-four hours of wall-clock time, most of it unattended.

## The headline numbers, and what the second one actually means

Fourteen of the twenty-three runs completed cleanly. Nine escalated — hit something that needed a human. That's a 39% escalation rate, which sounds high until you sit with the number next to it: **zero repeated root causes.** Every single escalation, across all twenty-three runs, traced back to a genuinely different underlying mechanism. Not the same bug wearing different clothes nine times — nine different things.

That distinction matters more than the raw completion rate. A framework that fails the same way repeatedly has a bug. A framework that fails nine different ways, each one caught and correctly routed to a human rather than silently producing wrong output, is a framework whose failure-handling is doing its job — the question worth asking isn't "why did 39% of runs need a person," it's "did the 39% get caught cleanly, and did the other 61% actually deserve to finish." Both turned out to be yes, and the rest of this piece is about how that got checked rather than assumed.

**Zero showstoppers** — nothing found that suggested the framework was broken at a foundational level. And after the runs finished, every one of the fourteen named findings got a second pass: reread against raw primary evidence rather than trusted as already correct. More on that at the end, because it's arguably the most important methodological fact about the whole exercise.

## The discipline underneath the numbers

None of the above is worth much without saying how it was actually watched, because the habit matters as much as the result. Every check-in during the burn-in followed the same small ritual: query the database fresh, don't assume yesterday's escalation list is still accurate; when something looks like a new escalation, verify it's genuinely new and genuinely blocking (a job-level escalation on an otherwise-active project isn't the same as the whole project stopping, and conflating the two wastes a false alarm); and when a finding depends on what a model claimed happened, go read the thing itself — the generated source file, the raw model completion, the actual database row — rather than trust the log's own summary of it. Several of the best findings in this corpus only exist because someone did the second thing instead of stopping at the first.

## A tour of what actually broke

Fourteen named findings came out of this corpus. Rather than list all of them, here are five, chosen because each shows something different about how a fleet of small models actually fails — and, in two cases, how well the recovery layers catch it.

### The same prompt is a coin flip, and the obvious fix would have been the wrong one

One doc — a small TOML parser — was run three times. Same design doc, byte-identical, same model doing the decomposition (DECOMPOSE). Two of the three times, DECOMPOSE invented an extra bead for a paired round-trip test (encode-then-decode should return the original value) that the doc's own numbered bead list didn't call for. The third time, working from the exact same document, it explicitly reasoned that yes, this invariant probably deserves its own bead — and then followed the doc's numbered list anyway. Reading the model's own stated reasoning across the three runs, it isn't guessing at random; it's weighing a genuinely close call and landing on different sides of it by chance.

The consequence wasn't symmetric. In the run where the invented bead got a slightly awkward name, everything still worked, just with an extra stall along the way. In the second run, the invented bead's test file happened to collide by name with another bead's — and that collision escalated the whole project. Same doc, same model, same decision point, and the outcome ranged from "fine" to "extra work" to "stopped cold," purely on which way a genuinely 50/50 judgment call fell.

The instinctive fix is "stop the model from ever adding a bead the doc's list doesn't call for" — which is exactly the discretion the previous piece in this series describes DECOMPOSE having removed, for a *different* mistake (merging two listed beads into one). Extending that same fix to cover *adding* an extra bead turned out to be the wrong move here: tracing the framework's own test suite for the mechanical check that governs this found a dedicated test asserting the opposite — that a legitimate, supplementary integration or invariant bead beyond the doc's numbered list is meant to pass, not fail. Blocking it would have quietly reversed a considered, already-tested decision. The actual gap is narrower and more specific: nothing yet stops an invented bead's test from colliding by name with a sibling's, which is the one thing that turned this particular coin flip into an escalation rather than just extra work. Smaller fix, correctly scoped — and a good demonstration of why this series keeps insisting on tracing a finding to the actual test suite before proposing what to do about it.

### A spec that quietly stops mid-sentence

A separate verb, REVISE_PENDING — the one that lightly revises the next pending bead's spec after the current one succeeds, "in light of what's now on disk" — occasionally handed back a spec that just... stopped. Not an error, not a timeout: a normal, well-formed response that happened to end mid-token, with the entire verbatim block of pinned literals sheared off the end. The model's own accounting said it finished normally; it simply produced less than it should have and nobody checked the length.

What makes this a real, fixable defect rather than background noise: it wasn't random across the corpus. On one specific bead — a densely-worded `cli` bead that appeared in two separate runs of the same doc — the exact same truncation happened both times, cut to the identical character count, ending on the identical fragment. That's not two independent coin flips; that's a deterministic response to something about that particular bead's prose that a stochastic-noise theory doesn't explain. Both times, the downstream execution stalled on the truncated fragment, and both times the framework's recovery path (a mechanical rebuild triggered by the stall) caught it and retried successfully — so this never became a visible escalation. It was only found at all because someone made a habit of diffing every revised spec against its predecessor for a length regression, rather than trusting that "the model said it succeeded" meant the output was complete.

### The bug the pipeline was built to catch, catching it

This one is worth walking through in full, because it's the cleanest demonstration in the whole corpus of the review architecture doing exactly its job. A fractal-rendering bead's execution ran for its full time budget without finishing, apparently unable to reconcile its own implementation against a failing downstream test. ADJUDICATE traced it — verifying a suspicion with an actual code check before concluding — to a different, already-completed bead: the SVG-path renderer had a "smart" behavior nobody's spec asked for, merging visually-contiguous line segments into a single continuous path instead of giving each one its own fresh path command, the way the design doc's own precisely worked example required. The renderer's own test never caught it, because its only test case was a single segment — the multi-segment behavior that would have exposed the bug simply was never exercised at the layer that introduced it.

Tracing further: the design doc's own pinned example for exactly this multi-segment case existed, correctly, but it lived under a different bead's section of the document — the one that uses the renderer, not the one that builds it — so the planning step never attached it to the bead whose code it actually constrains. The renderer bead was, in a real sense, never shown the one example that would have kept it from inventing an unrequested optimization. This routed correctly to a full stop and a human review — the review layer catching a genuine downstream defect that its own author's test suite had missed, exactly as intended.

Then the doc ran a second time, independently, with the same source document. And by pure chance — nothing was changed, this was the same stochastic variation that produced the coin-flip TOML result above — the planning step's second attempt at summarizing the renderer bead happened to phrase its own spec differently, landing the missing rule directly into the renderer's own prose this time instead of leaving it in the other bead's section. The multi-segment test case still wasn't written either way — the actual test coverage gap that let the bug through the first time was still there, unaddressed. But reading the literal generated source from the second run, line by line, the wrong "optimization" was simply never invented: every segment gets its own fresh path command, unconditionally, exactly as the doc requires. The one thing that changed between a run that shipped the bug and a run that didn't was whether the correct rule showed up in the one place a downstream model would actually read it — a controlled, if unplanned, natural experiment confirming that prose completeness at the level of the bead doing the work carries real, load-bearing weight, independent of whether a test exists to catch a violation.

### The most dramatic finding in the whole corpus

On the literal last bead of the entire twenty-three-run corpus, one single, objectively checkable fact triggered a five-round tug-of-war across four different verbs, and it's worth walking through cycle by cycle because the ending is the point.

The fact in question: does a task's completion status render, in the actual generated HTML, as the literal word "true" — or as a CSS class or a checkmark symbol, the way the design doc's own illustrative example happened to phrase it? These are different claims, and only one of them matches the real code.

- ADJUDICATE, reviewing a stalled execution, traced it directly against the running server's actual output and got it right: the real implementation renders the boolean as the literal string `"true"`, so the test needs to check for that.
- JUDGE's next cycle correctly adopted that verified fact.
- The cycle after that, JUDGE **reversed it** — reasoning its way back to the design doc's illustrative phrasing over the execution-verified ground truth from one cycle earlier, and instructing WRITE to check for a CSS class or checkmark that doesn't exist anywhere in the actual code.
- WRITE, faced with a contradiction it couldn't resolve, visibly thrashed — its own reasoning trace shows it explicitly re-deriving, in real time, that no such class or symbol exists anywhere in the template, proposing and discarding one compromise after another.
- CRITIQUE's next pass **independently re-derived the correct answer a third time**, unprompted, precisely matching the original verified fact.
- JUDGE **overrode that correct finding and approved the wrong test anyway** — its own stated reasoning claimed the review was being "over-literal" against the design doc's example, exactly reversing which side actually had the executable evidence.
- And then, with the test-writing budget exhausted and the wrong assertion locked in, EXECUTE — which has final, unilateral authority over the one file this bead is allowed to touch — simply rewrote the assertion to the correct one on its own initiative, before even running it, and shipped a passing result. ADJUDICATE verified the fix held and declared the bead done.

Final tally across the whole chain: one verb, JUDGE, was wrong twice on the exact same already-settled question, while every other verb that touched it — ADJUDICATE's execution-time diagnosis, JUDGE's own first-cycle review, CRITIQUE's independent re-derivation, and EXECUTE's final rewrite — got it right. And the outcome was: zero escalation, zero defect in the shipped code, about a half hour of extra churn on the very last bead the whole corpus had left to run. This is the layered-backstop architecture from the second piece in this series, caught in the act of doing exactly what it's for — not in a controlled test, but live, on the last opportunity the entire burn-in had to demonstrate it.

## The cost picture, in full

Across all twenty-three runs — roughly fifteen hundred completed model calls, on the order of eighty hours of summed compute — one single step accounts for **32.4% of the entire corpus's cost**: CRITIQUE, which reviews a freshly-written test before any implementation exists. It returns zero findings on 78% of its calls.

A close read of one representative bead's four REFINE cycles is the clearest illustration of what that 78% actually looks like from the inside. Across those four cycles, CRITIQUE surfaced roughly four distinct underlying gaps in the test's coverage. At least three of them were checkable by a plain literal-string search — does the test's assertions actually reference this exact HTML attribute, this exact button label, this exact template name the spec requires — the same category of check a design doc's own pin machinery already performs elsewhere in the pipeline. The fourth wasn't a real defect at all: the review step flagged the *implementation* for not existing yet, on a step that runs, by design, before any implementation is written. That specific mistake — confusing an intentional pre-implementation gap for a defect — recurred as its own distinct pattern three separate times across the whole corpus, independently.

Put plainly: of the most expensive single step in the entire pipeline, the corpus's best evidence says the overwhelming majority of what it actually catches doesn't need the open-ended reasoning it's currently spending ten-plus minutes per call doing. It needs a cheap check against the bead's own literal spec text, with the model's judgment reserved for what a literal check genuinely can't resolve.

## The last check: doubting the findings themselves

Here's the part that makes all of the above worth trusting rather than just another log full of confident claims. Once all twenty-three runs were done and all fourteen findings were written up, every one of them got re-examined from scratch, a second time, against raw primary sources — the actual git diff, the actual database rows, the actual generated source file — rather than accepted on the strength of the write-up's own prose.

Most held up exactly as written. A few came back sharper. One escalation's stated root cause — a model choosing the wrong recovery strategy for a stalled bead — turned out, once its own raw reasoning was read directly, to have diagnosed the underlying problem *correctly*; the actual gap was that the framework's guidance for that specific failure category simply didn't exist yet, so the model improvised with the playbook for a different, similar-looking failure instead. One suspected bug — the planning step apparently inventing an unauthorized extra bead — turned out, on tracing it back to the framework's own test suite, to be exactly the behavior a dedicated test asserted was correct; the fix that had been proposed for it would have quietly reversed a deliberate, already-tested design decision. Neither of those is a case of "the first pass was wrong" — both are cases of "the first pass was factually right and the second pass found a more precise or better-scoped way to say it." Across all fourteen, not one finding was overturned. The corrections that did surface were all refinements of mechanism or attribution, never reversals.

That's the same discipline the whole framework is built on, turned back on the exercise that was supposed to validate the framework — verify the account, don't trust it, even when the account is your own.

## Where this leaves things

The bottom line, stated the way it was stated at the time: the freeze is safe to lift. The recovery machinery — stalls, escalations, mechanical re-checks, the whole layered structure described across this series — consistently either recovers cleanly or correctly routes to a human, across every one of the nine genuinely distinct ways things went wrong. Nothing found rises to a foundational problem.

What changed is the priority order for what gets worked on next, and the burn-in's cost data is the reason. Chasing any one of the individual findings above would be a smaller win than what the corpus's aggregate numbers point at: the test-review step's cost-and-reliability profile, at 32% of all compute and mostly-mechanical yield, is both the single most expensive thing in the pipeline and, per the direct evidence, not a step whose pass/fail output can be trusted at face value without checking the specific case. That combination — expensive, and not cheaply trustworthy — is the strongest, best-evidenced argument this whole exercise produced, and it's next.
